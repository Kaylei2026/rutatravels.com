Premium ends at the aircraft door

By Ruta Travels 3 minute read

L.E.K. Consulting surveyed 1,800 premium leisure travelers in the United States this month and sorted them into five groups. The groups want different things. They agree on one complaint, and it is not about the seat.

The five segments the research names are affluent comfort-seekers, effortless empty-nesters, aspirational young families, strategic splurgers and independent explorers. Age and income, the study found, do not explain much on their own. What people are trying to get out of a trip explains more.

Then comes the part worth sitting with. Across all five groups, the same frustration surfaced: premium often does not feel worth what it cost. Neil Mittal, one of the report's authors, put it as travelers wanting noticeable improvements in quality, service, convenience or access that justify the price.

Four words, one of them unattended

Quality, service, convenience, access. Three of those have entire departments behind them.

Quality is seat pitch and mattress firmness and the weight of the cutlery. Service is training budgets and staffing ratios. Access is lounges, early boarding, the door that opens before the other door. Every airline and hotel group in the survey has people whose full job is one of those three.

Convenience sits there without an owner.

Not because nobody wants it. Because convenience is the only one of the four that does not live inside a single company's product. Quality happens in the seat. Service happens at the desk. Access happens at the door. Convenience happens in between, across a rental car company and a hotel and two airlines that have never spoken to each other, on a day when something moves.

The three hours nobody sold you

Consider a delay. Three hours, announced in stages, at a gate.

What you bought that morning was quality and service and access. The lie-flat seat is still lie-flat. The lounge still lets you in. The staff are still trained and still kind. None of that has failed.

What is failing is that you are now standing up, scrolling backwards through six months of email, trying to work out which of your bookings this touched. The connection, probably. Possibly the car. The front desk closes at ten and you are doing arithmetic in your head against a departures board.

That is the experience the survey is measuring when it reports that premium does not feel worth the price. The parts that were sold to you worked. The part that was never sold to anybody is the part that broke.

Why the industry keeps answering the wrong question

The report notes that Delta and Marriott drew the broadest appeal across segments, with Ritz-Carlton and Four Seasons generating strong advocacy inside particular ones. JetBlue scored well with younger travelers on clear pricing.

Every one of those wins is a win inside a company's own footprint. That is not a criticism of the brands. It is a description of what a brand can control. An airline cannot smooth the hour you spend at a rental counter. A hotel cannot know that your inbound moved.

So the industry responds to a convenience complaint with a quality answer, because quality is the thing it can actually build. Wider seat. Better amenity kit. Faster check-in for the segment that skews young. Each an improvement, none of them touching the three hours.

The seams between bookings belong to nobody, which is exactly why they stay rough. It is the same reason the water between Aruba and Bonaire is nobody's product.

What a traveler can do about it now

Book less separately than feels natural. Every additional confirmation is another thing that has to be found later, under pressure, on a phone.

Put the moving parts somewhere that is not your inbox. Email is a receipt archive, not a plan. It sorts by when things arrived rather than when they matter.

Know your own weak link before you travel. For most trips there is one booking that everything else leans on, usually a connection or a check-in time. When something shifts, that is the thing to check first.

And go in expecting the three hours. They are part of the trip whether or not anyone sold them to you. What decides how they feel is how quickly you can find out what they changed.
