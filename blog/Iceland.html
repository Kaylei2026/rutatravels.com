The summer you lose track of the hour

By Ruta Travels 4 minute read

You pull over somewhere past Vík because the light on the water is doing something you have not seen before, and when you get back in the car it is quarter past eleven at night. The sky has not changed. It will not change for another few weeks. An Icelandic summer removes the one signal every traveller quietly relies on, and without it the day stops telling you when to stop.

Around the solstice the dark never fully arrives. The sun sets late and barely, the sky dims into a long orange hour and comes back, and every cue your body uses to close a day is missing. You keep driving. You eat dinner at midnight. You wake at nine with the curtains blazing and no idea how you slept.

The ring road is a loop, not a straight line

Route 1 runs the whole coast, something over eight hundred miles, and the temptation is to divide the distance by the days and call it a plan. That arithmetic is wrong in both directions.

Weather rewrites the drive. A stretch that took ninety minutes in still air takes three hours in crosswind, and the wind here does things to car doors that rental companies have written specific clauses about. Single-lane bridges slow you. Gravel side roads slow you more. Then you find something unmarked and lose an hour standing in front of it.

The south coast carries most of the traffic and it earns it. Black sand, glacier tongues, the lagoon at Jökulsárlón with ice moving out to sea and washing back onto the beach in pieces. At Reynisfjara the waves come further up the sand than they look like they will, without warning, and people have died there. Watch the water rather than the phone.

The east is quieter. The north around Mývatn smells faintly of sulphur and feels like a different country. The Westfjords take days you probably do not have.

The interior is a separate decision. Highland F-roads open only when the thaw allows, usually somewhere in late June, and they legally require a proper four-wheel drive. Unmarked river crossings sit outside most rental insurance, which is a sentence worth reading twice before you attempt one.

Two things to check every morning

Conditions here change on a scale of hours. Locals check road conditions at road.is, the forecast at vedur.is and alerts at safetravel.is, and they do it before leaving rather than when something looks wrong through the windscreen.

Nothing else in the trip needs that kind of attention. Everything else was booked in March.

What breaks

The plan does. Not badly, and not all at once. A road closes, so the guesthouse two hundred miles ahead is now four hours further than it was at breakfast. The glacier walk moves to the afternoon. The afternoon thing has to go.

At that point the trip stops being a plan and becomes a search problem. The booking reference for tonight is in an email from a company whose name you have half forgotten. The tour operator's cancellation window was in the confirmation. Somebody in the car has the car hire agreement, in a photo, on a phone at four percent.

Travel has become extremely good at selling the pieces and has never solved holding them. Every part of an Iceland trip comes from a different company with its own confirmation format and its own idea of what a receipt looks like. When conditions are stable this is merely annoying. When the road closes, it is the whole problem.

And the diary

The strange effect of constant light is that the days lose their edges. Without dark between them a week runs together, and afterwards you cannot place which waterfall came before which, or whether the horses were on the second day or the fourth.

Write three lines each night, whatever hour that turns out to be. Where you stopped. What the weather did. One thing you would not have predicted.

That is the whole record, and in a place where every hour looks like the same hour, it is the only thing keeping them apart.
