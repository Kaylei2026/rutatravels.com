Three islands, thirty minutes apart

By Ruta Travels 4 minute read

Aruba, Bonaire and Curaçao sit in a line off the coast of Venezuela, close enough that the flight between any two of them is shorter than the taxi ride to the airport. On a map they read as one trip. In practice they are three, with two currencies, three sets of paperwork and a wind that does not stop, and the gap between them belongs to nobody.

The first thing you notice is the wind. It arrives before anything else, before the heat registers, before the light off the water, and it does not let up for the length of your stay. On Aruba the divi-divi trees have given up arguing with it. They lean southwest, every one of them, like a room of people turned toward the same door.

What each island asks of you

Aruba is the one that knows how to receive people. The hotel strip north of Oranjestad runs long and white, the restaurants keep menus in four languages, and the airport clears you through US customs before you board, so you land back home as a domestic arrival. If you have one week and no appetite for logistics, this is the island that will let you have it.

Bonaire wants you in the water. The whole coastline is a marine park, and the shore dive sites are marked by yellow-painted stones at the roadside, which means the plan for the day is a rental truck, a tank in the back, and a decision made at the last minute. Divers pay a nature fee before entering the water. Nobody arrives on Bonaire by accident.

Curaçao has a city. Willemstad's waterfront is a row of tall pastel houses along the Handelskade, and the pontoon bridge across the harbour swings open when a ship needs through, which strands you on whichever side you happened to be standing on. You wait. Somebody starts a conversation. Out on the west coast the beaches are small, tucked between cliffs, and the road to them is longer than the distance suggests.

Papiamento carries across all three. So does the wind.

The part nobody photographs

Here is where the trip gets complicated, and it is worth knowing before you book.

The islands are close, and the flights between them are limited. Schedules thin out midweek. A connection that looks like a formality on the map turns into a full day, or a night in a hotel you did not plan to pay for.

The money does not carry across either. Bonaire runs on US dollars. Aruba and Curaçao each have their own currency, and while dollars are taken most places, the change comes back in something else, and the exchange happens quietly in your favour or it does not.

Then there are the smaller things that arrive as separate emails, weeks apart. An entry form for one island. A nature tag for the water on another. A rental agreement with a scratch on the bumper you photographed at pickup and will need again at return. A dive shop confirmation. A ferry that runs on some days and not others.

None of it is difficult. All of it is scattered.

Why this keeps happening

The travel industry sells destinations. It does not sell the seams between them. Every island here has a tourism board, an airline route map and a hotel inventory, and each one is built to bring you to that island and keep you there. The stretch of water between Aruba and Bonaire is nobody's product, so nobody smooths it.

Multi-stop trips have grown faster than the tools for holding them. A single-destination holiday fits neatly into one booking confirmation. Three islands, two currencies, four ticket numbers and a permit fits nowhere, so it ends up living in a phone's email search bar, retrieved under pressure at a check-in desk with a queue behind you.

The fix is not more planning. It is having one place where the trip already sits, so the eleven things you booked in March are still together in July.

If you only have a week

Two islands rather than three. Fly into Curaçao for the city and the coves, take the short hop to Bonaire for the water, and let Aruba wait for its own trip. Go in the shoulder months if you can. The wind holds year round.

And keep something small from each day. A ferry ticket. The name of the reef. The bar where the bridge stranded you. Those are the parts that fade first, and they are the parts you will want back.
