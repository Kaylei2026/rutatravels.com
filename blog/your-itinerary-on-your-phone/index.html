Why your itinerary should live on your phone, not a company's server

By Ruta Travels 4 minute read

Most travel apps ask you to hand over your whole trip before they'll do anything useful. Create an account, upload your flights, and your plans go and live on someone else's computer. We made a different call, and it comes down to where your itinerary actually sits.

A trip is more personal than it looks written down. It's the exact dates you'll be in another city, the nights your house is dark, the hotel you're sleeping in, the flights you'll be unreachable on. Gathered in one place, that's a fairly complete picture of your life for a couple of weeks. It's worth being careful about who holds it.

What "on a server" quietly means

When your itinerary lives in a company's cloud, it's subject to things you don't control. A data breach can expose it. A quiet change to the privacy policy can start using it for ads. An acquisition can hand it to whoever bought the company, under whatever terms they write next. None of that requires anyone to be a villain. It just requires your data to be sitting somewhere you can't reach, governed by decisions made without you.

And travel data has a second life that most people never think about. Knowing you'll be in Lisbon from the ninth to the sixteenth is also knowing your home is empty those same nights. That's not a detail I want floating around in a database.

On your phone, it's simply yours

Ruta keeps your trip on your device. There's no account to create and nothing uploaded for us to read later. Your flights, hotels, cars, and notes are stored on the phone in your hand. If our company disappeared tomorrow, your itinerary would still be right there, working, because it never depended on us holding a copy.

I'll be honest about the tradeoff, because there is one. We can't see your data, which means we can't mine it to sell you things, and we also can't magically restore it if you lose your phone and never made a backup. So the responsibility shifts a little toward you: export your trip now and then, keep it somewhere safe. That's the deal. You get ownership, and ownership comes with a keychain.

The convenience that isn't free

Centralizing everyone's data is easier for the company, not the traveler. It makes syncing simpler, analytics richer, growth faster. Those are real benefits, but they land on the business side of the table. What you get handed in return is exposure, and you usually don't feel it until something goes wrong. Local-first is a bit more work for us to build. We think that's the correct place for the work to sit.

How to judge any travel app

You don't have to take our word for the principle. A few plain questions tell you most of what you need to know about any app holding your plans. Does it force an account before it will function at all? Where does it say your data is stored, and can you read that without a law degree? Is there a one-tap way to export everything and to delete everything? And if the company gets bought, what happens to what they already have on you?

Ask those before you pour a whole trip into something. The answers sort the tools quickly.

Keeping your itinerary on your phone won't make headlines. It's a small, deliberate choice that mostly shows up as an absence, the breach that never touches you, the ad that never follows you home from a trip you only planned. We think that quiet is worth building for.
